A save point is a promise disguised as an object. It says that the journey can pause here, that the player’s time matters, and that progress can survive the machine being switched off.

The earliest games made saving impossible or expensive. Passwords, battery-backed cartridges, and scarce save slots turned persistence into part of the design. We learned rituals around them: checking the icon, waiting for the chime, saving twice just in case.

Today, invisible autosaves are kinder and more convenient. But the old glowing typewriter, campfire, or crystal gave rest a physical shape. It made stopping feel like part of play rather than its interruption.
