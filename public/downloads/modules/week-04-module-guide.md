### The Evolution of Handheld Gaming

**REQUIRED**

YouTube | ~25 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cj5bNlXk17Q&ab\_channel=Boo](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cj5bNlXk17Q&ab_channel=Boo>)

Some highlights:

- Texas Instruments and Unisonic had calculators that could play games
- Mattel and Rockwell International released their first handheld electronic games in 1976 and was selling 500k units a week at one point
- Coleco (The Connecticut Leather Company) jumped into the handheld market, starting the handheld console wars
- Several other manufacturers started building similar devices
- Nintendo and Sharp released Game & Watch handheld consoles in 1980 after Gunpei Yokoi came up with the idea after watching someone play with a calculator on a train and they produced 50 different consoles over 10 years, selling millions of units
- The D-Pad that is still used today was introduced on the Donkey Kong Game & Watch
- Nintendo Game Boy launched in 1989, the first handheld console that could play different games using cartridges, sold 64.4 million units for $90 each (BTW, Tetris was bundled with the units)
- Several competitors came out, but were more expensive, lower quality, and didn't sell as well
- Game Boy Color came out in 1998
- Today, Game Boy series is third best selling console of all time
- Bandai (now Bandai Namco, which owns Pac-Man, Tekken, Dark Souls, Elden Ring) released a competitor that became the second highest selling handheld console in Japan at the time
- PlayStation Portable released in 2004 right after Nintendo release the DS, both marvels and sold well, kicking off serious competition between Sony and Nintendo in the handheld market, but Nintendo won with the 3DS
- Nintendo continues to dominate the market, now with Nintendo Switch 2
- Valve Steam Deck allows people to play desktop PC games on a handheld, and there are other devices that could be thought of as competitors, but Nintendo is far ahead of them

- LO 1: Students will be able to explain the historical development of video games, from early arcade games to contemporary digital gaming platforms.

### The History, Evolution, and Future of Mobile Gaming

**REQUIRED**

Article | ~20 min

[https://gameopedia.com/blogs/the-history-evolution-and-future-of-mobile-gaming](<https://gameopedia.com/blogs/the-history-evolution-and-future-of-mobile-gaming>)

Highlights:

- Mobile gaming projected to be $139.5 billion industry by 2026
- Tetris was the first game released on a mobile phone, but Snake on a Nokia phone in 1997 was when mobile phone games started to get traction
- In 2001, Space Invaders, an arcade great, was released for mobile phones
- Apple released iPhone in 2007 and launched AppStore in 2008, which started the modern mobile game revolution
- Angry Birds came out in 2009 and when they ported it to Android, they released both paid and ad-supported free versions
- Farmville on Facebook had over 80 million players by February 2010
- Candy Crush Saga released in October 2012, also featuring Facebook integration, advertising, and in-app purchases, earning revenues of $1.88 billion by the end of 2013
- Clash of Clans was earning $5.15 million per day by September 2014
- Cross-play between mobile, PC, and consoles is a growing trend, with Fortnite, Genshin Impact, and Among Us as examples
- Almost 83 billion mobile games were downloaded in 2021, 64% of people who play mobile games do so on a daily basis
- Today, 4% of apps on Android Play Store are paid
- Temple Run released for $0.99 in 2011, but switched to Free to Play after one month, increasing revenue by 10x through in-app purchases
- Popular monetization models today: (1) Paid games (one-time payment), (2) Free to play with in-game transactions, (3) Free to play with advertisements, (4) Subscriptions
- Proceeds to discuss popular mobile gaming categories, trends, and future looking information

### Mass market, mystery boxes and metric-driven design: The legacy of FarmVille

**REQUIRED**

Article | ~14 min

[https://www.gamesindustry.biz/mass-market-mystery-boxes-and-metric-driven-design-the-legacy-of-farmville](<https://www.gamesindustry.biz/mass-market-mystery-boxes-and-metric-driven-design-the-legacy-of-farmville>)

This article is not about portable gaming, but about the free to play model and monetization strategies that have become common in mobile games, so I thought it would be worth sharing. Highlights:

- Discusses the casual farming simulation game FarmVille, played in a Web browser (within Facebook), that became a huge success
- Produced in six weeks, had hit 1 million players within 5 days!
- Free to play
- Encouraged players to promote the game to their social network within Facebook
- Players who were playing every day didn't consider themselves gamers
- Led to the explosion of social and free-to-play games
- At one point had 80 million daily players
- To pay the bills, they introduced an in-game currency purchased with real money that was then used to buy in-game items
- Introduced daily bonuses, free items given to players for logging in each day, a common mechanic in many video games today
- Introduced "mystery boxes" (now commonly known as loot boxes) that granted players a random reward - there is a lot of scrutiny today over whether the modern implementations of loot boxes constitute gambling
- Shut down in 2020
- "showed developers, investors and most importantly players that there was a world of games that could appeal to anyone"

### Free To Share Games Just Got SERIOUS

**REQUIRED**

YouTube | ~16 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rbhBsZwiJIM](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rbhBsZwiJIM>)

Highlights: 

- Split Fiction is an example of a relatively new idea: free to share games, where one person purchases a license and can share free access to another person (or other people) that they will play with
- Split Fiction is 2-person co-op - it requires two players and only two players to play together
- A handful of other games have been released under this model
- Sunderfolk takes the concept in a new direction, where one person buys and runs the full game and several others control their characters via a free mobile app, similar to how a group of friends might share a tabletop game or board game - one person buys it and everyone plays together *(I've played this game and it's a lot of fun, and I think this is a really interesting model that could be a great way to get more people playing together)*
- Likens the model to the Jackbox games
- The video points out that a game like R.E.P.O. is $10 per person but if you have 5 friends playing together, that is essentially the same price as Sunderfolk

### New York Targets Valve's Loot Boxes as Illegal Gambling

**EXPLORE FURTHER**

Article

[https://www.joneswalker.com/en/insights/blogs/perspectives/new-york-targets-valves-loot-boxes-as-illegal-gambling.html?id=102ml3c](<https://www.joneswalker.com/en/insights/blogs/perspectives/new-york-targets-valves-loot-boxes-as-illegal-gambling.html?id=102ml3c>)

This article examines a groundbreaking legal challenge brought by the New York Attorney General against Valve Corporation, alleging that the company’s randomized loot boxes function as an unauthorized gambling operation. While past private lawsuits failed because courts viewed in-game items as lacking measurable monetary worth, this new litigation argues that Valve's virtual "skins" are convertible to real-world cash through both official and third-party marketplaces. The author highlights how New York’s specific penal laws and Valve's alleged facilitation of secondary markets create a unique threat to the gaming industry’s traditional monetization models. Ultimately, the article serves to warn developers that the legal immunity surrounding digital prizes is shifting, especially regarding systems that utilize psychological triggers to encourage spending among minor children.
