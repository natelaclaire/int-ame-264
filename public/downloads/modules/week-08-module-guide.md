### What Makes Great Learning Games?

**REQUIRED**

YouTube | ~16 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6uXUyBEAWR0&ab\_channel=FilamentGames](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6uXUyBEAWR0&ab_channel=FilamentGames>)

Dan Norton of Filament Games discusses his company's approach to designing effective learning games, stemming from over a decade of experience. He outlines critical initial steps and three core strategies for translating learning objectives into engaging gameplay.

* The first crucial steps are to clearly **define learning objectives** and then **establish assessments** to measure the intended impact, ensuring that the game effectively teaches what it aims to and that success can be objectively determined.  
* Filament Games uses three core strategies to integrate learning into mechanics: **Identity** (asking players to step into a role, like a scientist, to see the world from a new perspective or embody a professional practice); **Verbs** (creating digital actions or metaphors for doing things, like breeding plants using Punnett squares, to provide practice on specific skills); and **Systems** (designing rules that govern interactions in a complex world, like a plant's anatomy, to teach systems thinking and how parts work together).  
* A key warning is to **avoid making learning content feel like an "unfortunate tax"** that needs to be "concealed" by the game; instead, the learning objective itself should be an exciting and interesting problem with a clear purpose and context for the learner's life.

This paper details the MADRIGALE project, which aims to design and develop an action game prototype specifically for dyslexic children to improve reading skills. ### More Than Just Fun and Games: The Longitudinal Relationships Between Strategic Video Games, Self-Reported Problem Solving Skills, and Academic Grades

**EXPLORE FURTHER**

Article | ~80 min

[https://maine.primo.exlibrisgroup.com/permalink/01MAINE\_INST/1oa9cn5/cdi\_proquest\_miscellaneous\_1429629833](<https://maine.primo.exlibrisgroup.com/permalink/01MAINE_INST/1oa9cn5/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_1429629833>)

This empirical research study investigates the longitudinal relationship between strategic video game play, self-reported problem-solving skills, and academic grades among adolescents over the four years of high school. It addresses the gap in research on positive outcomes of video game play, contrasting with prior focus on negative effects like addiction and aggression.

* The study found that **more strategic video game play (role-playing and strategy games) predicted higher self-reported problem-solving skills over time** compared to less strategic or fast-paced video game play. Strategic games, like *Splinter Cell* or *World of Warcraft*, encourage players to gather information, weigh options, and formulate strategies before acting, which enhances problem-solving skills.  
* Results supported an **indirect association between strategic video game play and academic grades**: strategic video game play predicted higher self-reported problem-solving skills, which, in turn, predicted higher academic grades. Fast-paced games (fighting, action, racing) did not show this positive link.  
* The research specifically supported a **cognitive training effect** (playing strategic video games leads to an increase in problem-solving skills), but **did not find support for a selection effect** (individuals with better problem-solving skills are more likely to play strategic games); in fact, higher problem-solving skills predicted *less* strategic video game play in a later grade, possibly due to self-selection into academic activities.

### Jim Gee Principles on Gaming

**EXPLORE FURTHER**

YouTube | ~24 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4aQAgAjTozk&ab\_channel=ChrisThorn](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4aQAgAjTozk&ab_channel=ChrisThorn>)

James Paul Gee outlines 13 principles that good video games employ to foster effective learning, problem-solving, and deep understanding, suggesting these principles can be applied to educational design. He categorizes these principles into three areas: empowered learners, problem-based learning, and deep understanding.

* **Empowered Learners:** Games empower learners through the **Agent/Co-Design principle** (players influence the game and feel like agents), **Customization principle** (allowing different play styles and safe experimentation), **Identity principle** (taking on roles with clear goals, like a scientist or adventurer), and **Manipulation principle** (fine control over the game world, deeply engaging body and mind with learning tools).  
* **Problem-Based Learning:** Games excel at problem-solving via the **Sequencing Problems principle** (levels teaching progressively harder solutions), **Pleasantly Frustrating principle** (challenging but achievable problems leading to "flow"), **Cycle of Expertise principle** (practicing routine knowledge, then challenging it with "boss battles" to ratchet up mastery), **Just-in-Time/On-Demand Information principle** (giving information when needed), **Fish Tank principle** (simplifying complex systems in early levels, gradually adding complexity), **Sandboxes principle** (safe spaces for exploration and risk-taking without immediate judgment), and **Skills Under Strategies principle** (practicing basic skills within meaningful problem-solving contexts).  
* **Deep Understanding:** Games create deep understanding through **System Thinking principle** (building mental models of complex interacting rules, fostering scientific reasoning) and **Situated Meaning principle** (associating words/symbols with images, actions, goals, and experiences rather than just definitions).

### Your brain on video games \| Daphne Bavelier

**EXPLORE FURTHER**

TED Talk | ~18 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FktsFcooIG8&ab\_channel=TED](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FktsFcooIG8&ab_channel=TED>)

Daphne Bavelier, a brain scientist, shares her research on the **positive effects of action-packed shooter video games** on the brain, challenging common negative perceptions about screen time. She emphasizes leveraging the power of pervasive video gaming for education and rehabilitation.

* Action video games can **significantly improve vision**, enhancing the ability to resolve small details in clutter and distinguish different levels of gray, leading to vision "better than normal" players. This research is being leveraged to develop games for patients with low vision.  
* Playing action games **does not lead to attention problems; rather, it improves attention**, allowing faster resolution of cognitive conflicts (like the Stroop effect) and enhancing the ability to track multiple objects simultaneously. Brain imaging reveals that networks controlling attention (parietal cortex, frontal lobe, anterior cingulate) become **more efficient** in action game players.  
* Bavelier clarifies that **not all media are created equal**: action games have distinct positive effects on cognition and perception, contrasting sharply with high multimedia-tasking, which negatively impacts multitasking skills. She likens the beneficial "ingredients" in action games to those in wine (in reasonable doses), stating that these ingredients are powerful for brain plasticity, learning, and attention, and research aims to extract them for better educational and rehabilitation games.

### Create Educational Games with TinyTap

**EXPLORE FURTHER**

YouTube | ~17 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5GkZZ97\_CR8&ab\_channel=TechnologyforTeachersandStudents](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5GkZZ97_CR8&ab_channel=TechnologyforTeachersandStudents>)

This video demonstrates how to create educational games using TinyTap, a tool accessible via its website. It covers searching for existing games, but emphasizes the ability for users to create their own custom learning experiences.

* TinyTap provides various **creation packs** (themes, shapes, frames) and tools for **uploading images, searching for clip art/animations, and adding text** to design game screens or "levels".  
* Users can build different interactive activity types, including **"Ask a Question"** (recording questions and outlining correct answers), **"Puzzle"** (tracing shapes to create puzzle pieces), **"Sound Board"** (linking sounds to objects), and **"Talk or Type"** (tracing an area for a text input with correct answers).  
* Once created, games can be **published publicly** with a name, description, category, language, and age range, generating a shareable URL or embed code for easy access by others, even without a TinyTap account.

### Press Play -- Gaming, Simulation & Achievement in the Classroom: Jonathon Best at TEDxDenverTeachers

**EXPLORE FURTHER**

TED Talk | ~17 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lKpo5SeZWns&ab\_channel=TEDxTalks](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lKpo5SeZWns&ab_channel=TEDxTalks>)

Jonathon Best, an educator, proposes integrating simulation and gaming into the classroom to drive motivation and performance. He suggests that since many students already live in "highly simulated" worlds outside of school, denying this presence in the classroom can alienate them.

* Simulation-based learning aims to allow students to **connect to complex material through imagined or simulated experiences**. Examples include creating **classroom avatars** to dismiss negative baggage and experience risk differently (like Mario falling into a fire pit, you just restart).  
* Other forms of simulation-based learning include **Discovery-based instruction**, thematically designing units around **quests or challenges** (e.g., defeating an evil boss), and implementing **in-class achievement systems** (like Xbox/PlayStation achievements) to celebrate successes collectively.  
* Best emphasizes a **"vernacular shift"** (e.g., an exit ticket becomes a "boss battle") and daily tracking of student/avatar progress to provide ownership and engagement. He believes this approach can make education more equitable and liberating by meeting students where they are.
