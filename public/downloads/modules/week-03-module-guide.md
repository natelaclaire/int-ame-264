A couple terms I want to quickly clarify:

**RGB**

RGB stands for Red, Green, and Blue — the primary light colors used to generate images on digital displays.

**Vector Graphics**

Vector graphics are images created mathematically using lines, curves, and shapes rather than individual pixels.
This differs from raster graphics, which are built pixel-by-pixel.

Vector graphics scale smoothly without losing quality, while raster graphics are better suited for detailed photographic imagery.

### Atari: Game Over

**REQUIRED**

YouTube | 66 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=03oC6-zPZHQ](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=03oC6-zPZHQ>)

One of the biggest pieces this week is the documentary *Atari: Game Over*.

This documentary focuses on:

- the infamous E.T. video game,
- the 1983 video game crash,
- and the urban legend surrounding buried Atari cartridges in the desert.

A few key takeaways:

**The E.T. Game Was Made in Five Weeks**

That timeline is astonishing.

Even for the early 1980s, similar games normally took several months to produce.

Howard Scott Warshaw — the game’s designer — completed the project under extreme pressure.

The documentary makes a strong argument that the real problem wasn’t the developer, but the unrealistic business decisions surrounding the project.

**The Burial Was Real**

Yes — Atari really did bury cartridges in a landfill.

But it turns out it wasn’t just E.T. cartridges. Many other Atari materials were buried as well.

The documentary follows archaeologists and historians who tracked down the site and excavated it.

**The Crash Was Bigger Than One Game**

The E.T. game did not single-handedly destroy the industry.

Instead, it became a symbol of larger problems:

- rushed production,
- poor quality control,
- oversaturation,
- and unrealistic expectations.

A rendering optimization technique that allowed games to only draw what the player could actually see.

id Software openly encouraged players to modify their games.

**Shareware Distribution**

Instead of relying entirely on retail stores, id distributed portions of games freely and encouraged players to share them.

This became an incredibly successful grassroots marketing strategy.

### Let's Begin Again: Sierra On-Line and the Origins of the Graphical Adventure Game

**EXPLORE FURTHER**

Article

[https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1166784.pdf](<https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1166784.pdf>)

Laine Nooney’s scholarly article reexamines the origins of Sierra On-Line by challenging the traditional, linear "evolutionary narrative" that dominates video game history. The text argues that the company’s first hit, Mystery House, is often oversimplified as a mere technical stepping stone or a rival to text-based fiction, rather than being understood through its material and domestic realities. By detailing the collaborative dynamic between Roberta and Ken Williams, the author highlights how Roberta’s visual design process and Ken’s technical ingenuity converged to pioneer the graphical adventure genre. Ultimately, Nooney utilizes a counter-historical methodology to show that Sierra’s emergence was not an inevitable march of progress, but a complex result of specific technological conditions and human affect within their California home.

### Classic Postmortem: The Making Of Myst

**EXPLORE FURTHER**

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7cULHgP8tmo](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7cULHgP8tmo>)

In this retrospective presentation, Robin Miller details the serendipitous creation of Myst, a landmark title that prioritized nonlinear storytelling and explorable environments over traditional gaming conventions. Developed on a modest budget using HyperCard, the project was born from a desire to create an immersive world for adults where the player acts as the true protagonist without the distance of an avatar. The brothers navigated significant technical limitations of early CD-ROM technology by using pre-rendered 3D graphics and "ages" to manage memory, while their design philosophy focused on non-arbitrary puzzles that felt like natural parts of the environment. Ultimately, Miller attributes the game's massive success to a pure creative process fueled by personal curiosity and rigorous observational play-testing, rather than market projections or established industry trends.
