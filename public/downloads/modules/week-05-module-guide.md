## Representation, Inclusion, and Accessibility

Analyzing representation and inclusivity in gaming.

This week we analyze representation and inclusivity in gaming, discussing how different groups are portrayed and the efforts to create a more inclusive gaming environment, including the role of features that make games more accessible to all players. This includes assistive technologies and design principles. We will also take a look at character design, storylines, representation among the game development community, and community dynamics.

### Tropes vs. Women in Video Games

**REQUIRED**

YouTube Playlist | 83 min

[https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLn4ob\_5\_ttEaZWIYcx7VKiFheMSEp1gbq](<https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLn4ob_5_ttEaZWIYcx7VKiFheMSEp1gbq>)

Some highlights:

* **Female Sidekicks and Companions:**  
  * Female companions, like Elizabeth in Bioshock Infinite, often serve significant narrative roles but are reduced to basic gameplay functions, such as being a "glorified door opener".  
    * Despite possessing powers, they are mechanically limited to menial tasks and can feel like tools or "sexualized slot machines".  
    * Yorda in ICO is a quintessential example of the **"damsel escort mission,"** where a largely helpless female character accompanies the player, often feeling like a burden requiring protection and assistance to traverse the environment (like Ashley Graham in Resident Evil 4 or Ellie needing help crossing water in The Last of Us). \- ***I’ve watched the gameplay of RE:4 (not played it myself) and been so bothered by Ashley’s role…***  
    * The pattern of men helping or carrying women in these situations reinforces sexist ideas about men as protectors and women needing protection.  
    * Female characters can also be used as rudimentary tools in gameplay (e.g., Princess Ruto as a switch weight). In Metal Gear Solid 5, a woman is one of four sidekick options and the others are all non-human.  
    * Female companions often act as "cheerleaders," boosting the player's ego, interactions that are rarely mutually supportive. They are typically not designed for players to develop genuine relationships with.  
    * Competent female companions whose skills are equal to the player (Tess in The Last of Us, female squad members in Gears of War) challenge harmful ideas.  
    * More games are subverting these patterns by providing supporting characters who feel like separate individuals with their own feelings and desires (One Night Stand, Riley in The Last Of Us: Left Behind).  
    * When female supporting characters lack depth and exist primarily to be protected or ordered around, they reinforce harmful ideas and fail as characters; games should reflect that people are more than tools or burdens.  
  * **Body Diversity in Video Games:**  
    * Lack of diversity in female body types is common across games like Ultra Street Fighter IV, League of Legends, and Dota 2, where male characters exhibit a wide range of body shapes and proportions, while female characters are predominantly slender, often with prominent breasts, representing a standard of conventional attractiveness.  
    * Male characters' physiques often communicate their personality or abilities, but female characters are frequently limited to a single body type designed for sexual appeal to presumed straight male players.  
    * This limited representation is harmful as it links women's value to their desirability to men and reinforces narrow cultural beauty standards.  
    * The absence of diverse female bodies reinforces the false idea that women who don't match the cultural beauty standard are less valuable.  
    * This creativity limitation also affects age representation; older male heroes are common, but playable female characters are almost always young, again tied to sexual appeal, suggesting women's value decreases with age.  
    * While positive examples of female characters with different body types or ages exist (Alyssa in Life is Strange, Agnes MacBean in Assassin's Creed Syndicate, Kreia in Knights of the Old Republic II), they are rare in major contemporary games.  
    * Later additions to Overwatch like Mei, Zarya (different body types), and Ana (older woman of color) are encouraging steps, but developers must actively work towards the same diversity for female bodies as for male characters.  
    * Liberating female characters from narrow beauty standards can make games more interesting and help portray all women as valuable, autonomous individuals.  
  * **Animation and Inclusion Challenges:**  
    * Including female combatants is preferable to their absence. Violence against women in games is problematic when they are specifically victimized for being women. When women are active participants on equal footing, fighting them (like in Street Fighter) is not violence against women.  
    * However, female combatants are often sexualized, making attacks against them feel like gendered violence (e.g., Saints Row The Third's "Whored Mode," Wolfenstein's Elite Guard, Hitman Absolution's Saints, Metal Gear Solid 4's Beauty & the Beast unit). Violence against female characters should not be "sexy".  
    * Female combatants should be presented simply as combatants alongside men on equal footing, without sexualization, as seen with female police in Bioshock Infinite or gang members in Assassin's Creed Syndicate.  
  * **Movement and the Male Gaze:**  
    * Character movement can reinforce or resist gender stereotypes. In Destiny, male and female characters' sitting animations differ: male characters sit simply, suggesting confidence, while female characters sit in a delicate, stylized manner.  
    * Movement is used to communicate personality and skills. However, female characters' movement is often used to exude sexuality for the presumed straight male player.  
    * This sexualizing walk pattern is a manifestation of the **male gaze**, a concept where visual arts are structured around a presumed masculine viewer.  
    * The solution is not equal sexualization of men.  
    * There is potential for female characters to be animated emphasizing personality traits like strength or grace, rather than being framed as sex objects (e.g., Ellie in The Last of Us). Equality means treating all characters as full human beings, not reducing them to objectified parts.  
  * **Sexualized and Impractical Clothing:**  
    * Clothing is a primary way designers communicate a character's importance. While female heroes are often in dangerous situations requiring practical gear, their outfits are frequently sexualized and absurdly impractical (e.g., Ivy in Soulcalibur, Cammy in Street Fighter, Jessica Sherawat in Resident Evil: Revelations).  
    * This contributes to the **hyper-sexualization** of female characters, valuing them primarily for sexual characteristics.  
    * Conflating sexualization with power is harmful, suggesting women's power comes from being desirable to men and that empowered women's sexuality is always available to men. This myth encourages believing sexualization is the route to equality.  
    * Solving this problem is not difficult, and some games (Dark Souls, XCOM, Assassin's Creed Syndicate) use practical armor/clothing for female characters.  
    * Impractical clothing encourages viewing female characters as sex objects. Practical clothing and non-sexualized expressions of sexuality encourage viewing them as people.  
  * **Exotification and Racism:**  
    * Far Cry 3 features Citra, a woman of color presented as a tribal warrior goddess who embodies a blend of sexist and racist stereotypes, functioning as a sexual motivator/reward for the player while her mystical powers and body paint evoke harmful notions about tribal cultures and women of color.  
    * This is an example of **exotification**, treating a group as inherently different, alluring, and strange, often involving sexual fetishization based on false racial notions.  
    * The Diablo III witch doctor, represented by black characters, relies on visual stereotypes of tribal warriors, perpetuating centuries-old racist imagery. Fighting games like Street Fighter IV feature characters like Elena who combine sexualization with vague suggestions of tribal culture and stereotypical mystical traits.  
    * These depictions are cultural appropriation, reducing complex cultures to stereotypical signifiers used as shorthand for "exotic," reinforcing damaging myths that contribute to the marginalization of women of color today.  
    * Respectful portrayals of women of color whose cultural backgrounds are acknowledged are rare. While some games feature black male protagonists or acknowledge racism (Watchdogs 2, Mafia 3), positive examples for women of color are limited.  
    * Games like 1979 Revolution and Never Alone show how cultural history can be incorporated respectfully, counteracting stereotypes and enriching player understanding. Games should strive for thoughtful, humanizing representations of people of color.  
  * **Sinister Seductress/Demonization of Femaleness:**  
    * Alternatively, female characters' attractiveness or seductiveness can make them threatening, as seen with mythological Sirens or characters like Morinth in Mass Effect 2, whose mating is deadly.  
    * This "temptress" tradition includes characters whose sexuality is presented as a threat or weapon (Dark Queen, Elizebet, Layla), rooted in ancient misogynistic ideas about women being deceptive and evil.  
    * Games sometimes combine these types, initially presenting attractive female characters whose true forms are revealed as monstrous (Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons, DmC, Ms. Splosion Man). This implies female sexuality is deceptive or threatening.  
    * When male heroes defeat these characters, the victory is often gendered, reasserting male dominance. Killings can be graphically sexualized.  
    * It is possible to have female villains whose evil isn't tied to gender or sexuality (Kreia, GLaDOS, Carmen Sandiego).  
    * Using these tropes uncritically perpetuates harmful, false notions about women being misleading, manipulative, and that female sexuality should be feared and controlled.

### The Power of Play: The Portrayal and Performance of Race in Video Games

**REQUIRED**

Book excerpt | 63 min

[https://com427spring2017ncsu.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/2007-everett\_\_watkins-powerofplay-raceingames.pdf](<https://com427spring2017ncsu.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/2007-everett__watkins-powerofplay-raceingames.pdf>)

An excerpt from [The Ecology of Games: Connecting Youth, Games, and Learning](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262693646/the-ecology-of-games/), edited by Katie Salen. 

Highlights:

* Titles like *GTA* simulate gritty urban environments with black and Latino characters.  
  * These games often rely on exaggerated stereotypes: hypermasculinity, criminality, and exoticism.  
  * Developers market these elements as “authentic” urban culture, even though they draw from pre-existing media stereotypes.  
  * RPZs are game environments where racial messages are taught through interaction, not just representation.  
  * The authors explore how gameplay—such as missions and role-playing—reinforces ideas about race.  
  * *GTA*, in particular, is criticized for presenting black and Latino characters in environments that normalize violence and deviance.  
  * The concept of **“digital minstrelsy”** describes how players perform race through gameplay—similar to blackface in minstrel shows.  
  * Players, especially white players, are invited to inhabit racialized characters and act out racialized behaviors for entertainment.  
  * **Character roles**: Black and Latino characters are more visible in these games, but often as gang members, athletes, or criminals.  
  * **Gender**: Black and Latina women are typically portrayed as hypersexualized, victims, or accessories to male characters.  
  * **Sound and setting**: Audio and environment design (graffiti, gunfire, hip-hop music) contribute to a “realistic” portrayal of urban chaos.  
  * Games can reinforce harmful stereotypes, even while appearing “authentic.”  
  * To shift this, there must be broader access to game creation tools and more racial diversity in the game industry.  
  * Addressing race in games isn’t just about changing content—it’s about changing who gets to create it.

### Accessible Design VS Accessibility Settings - Access-Ability

**REQUIRED**

YouTube | 10 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oYXR6zLEjPk](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oYXR6zLEjPk>)

While high-profile titles often boast a massive list of accessibility settings to fix problems after a game is built, this source argues that true progress lies in accessible design, where inclusivity is baked into the core experience from day one. Rather than relying solely on optional filters or toggles, developers are increasingly using inherent design choices—such as adding unique shapes to color-coded puzzles or utilizing spatial audio—to ensure the game is naturally playable for everyone without requiring extra adjustments. This shift represents a move toward proactive development over reactive solutions, suggesting that the sheer number of menu options is an incomplete metric for how welcoming a game actually is to disabled players. Ultimately, the goal is to balance customizable features with a baseline of universal access, moving the industry toward a future where games are fundamentally built to be inclusive by default.

### Accessibility of Communications in Video Games

**EXPLORE FURTHER**

Article | 6 min

[https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/accessibility-communications-video-games](<https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/accessibility-communications-video-games>)

The Twenty-First Century Communications and Video Accessibility Act mandates that advanced communications services, such as voice and text chat within video games, must be accessible and usable for individuals with disabilities.
