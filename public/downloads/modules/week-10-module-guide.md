### A Trip Down Memory Lane: The Origins of Game Modding

**REQUIRED**

YouTube | ~17 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xIaYCcAViqk&ab\_channel=Woahvi](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xIaYCcAViqk&ab_channel=Woahvi>)

This video delves into the history of game modding, tracing its roots back to the 1980s with early examples like *Castle Wolfenstein*. It highlights how the modding community evolved, particularly with id Software's *Doom* being the first game to offer built-in mod support. The video also discusses how many popular games, such as *Team Fortress* and *Counter-Strike*, originally started as mods, showcasing the significant impact of fan creations on the gaming industry.

*Highlights:*  

  * Early modding involved fans creating new content for games like *Castle Wolfenstein* (e.g., "Castle Smurfenstein") in the early 1980s, often through reverse-engineering game files.  
  * id Software's *Doom* (1993) revolutionized modding by being the first game to include built-in mod support through "wad" files, which made it easy for players to modify game assets and share their creations.  
  * Many successful, standalone games originated as mods, demonstrating modding's role as a "genesis of the Battle Royale craze" (e.g., *DayZ Battle Royale* leading to *PUBG*) and other major genres.

### A Lawyer Explains the PointCrow Takedowns and the Legality of Mods

**REQUIRED**

YouTube | ~25 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mo\_AmQgSSqY&ab\_channel=MoonChannel](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mo_AmQgSSqY&ab_channel=MoonChannel>)

In this video, a lawyer, Mooney, explains the legal perspective on game modding, using Nintendo's copyright strikes against YouTuber PointCrow as a case study. The video clarifies that most mods are considered copyright infringement because they are "derivative works" of the original game, and the "fair use" defense is often difficult to apply in this context. It emphasizes that copyright holders, especially large companies like Nintendo, fiercely protect their intellectual property.

*Highlights:*  

  * Legally, virtually all mods are considered copyright infringement under US law because they are "derivative works" of the original game, and copyright holders have exclusive rights to create such works.  
  * The "fair use" defense, which allows limited use of copyrighted material, is a litigation defense (used only in court) and is generally difficult to apply to game mods, especially those that extensively transform a game while retaining its core identity.  
  * Game content guidelines provided by companies (like Nintendo's) are described as "benevolent offerings" and "not a license agreement," meaning the company can unilaterally decide what content is acceptable and withdraw its toleration at any time.

### Rolling Your Own (from Pastplay: Teaching and Learning History with Technology)

**REQUIRED**

Book Excerpt | ~24 min

[https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/166/oa\_edited\_volume/chapter/1127334](<https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/166/oa_edited_volume/chapter/1127334>)

This academic chapter explores the use of modding commercial games, specifically *Civilization IV*, for educational purposes in history. The author recounts his unsuccessful attempt to impose a mod-based assignment in his online Roman history class due to the "creepy treehouse" phenomenon (forced, unnatural technology use). In contrast, he observes how genuine, self-organized learning about history occurs within online fan modding communities like Civfanatics, often "without teachers".

*Highlights:*  

  * Modding offers potential for historical education by allowing students to engage with complex historical scenarios, causality, and contingency (e.g., the Year of the Four Emperors in *Civilization IV*).  
  * The author's experience demonstrates the "creepy treehouse" phenomenon, where forced or unnatural integration of technology in education can hinder engagement. Successful educational modding, he argues, should be student-led and emerge naturally from community interest.  
  * Online fan communities can be vibrant spaces for self-organized learning, where members collaboratively explore and debate historical authenticity in their mods, providing a model for educators.

### Do Private Servers Benefit Or Harm MMORPGs?

**REQUIRED**

Article | ~8 min

[https://mmos.com/editorials/do-private-servers-benefit-or-harm-mmorpgs](<https://mmos.com/editorials/do-private-servers-benefit-or-harm-mmorpgs>)

This opinion piece argues that private servers, while legally precarious, often benefit MMORPGs by preserving older game experiences, fostering dedicated communities, and generating continued interest in intellectual properties. The author shares personal anecdotes about playing on private *Ragnarok Online* servers and discusses instances where official developers have either tolerated or formally sanctioned fan projects.

*Highlights:*  

  * Private servers often exist because players desire a "return to glory days" or a specific gameplay experience (e.g., different experience rates, level caps) that official servers no longer provide or have diverged from (e.g., "vanilla World of Warcraft").

  * The author argues that private servers often bolster interest in the original IP, acting as a form of "additional advertising" and "influencer support," even if some (especially profit-driven ones) face legal action from publishers.

### One QR Code Destroyed The 3DS Forever

**EXPLORE FURTHER**

YouTube | ~27 min

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=\_zuuVwUPKac](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_zuuVwUPKac>)

This final video is slightly off-topic, but somewhat related. I came across it recently and wished I had found and shared it when we were discussing portable gaming, but since it relates to hacking game platforms I figured I could share it when we discuss game modding since some game modding does involve hacking. It recounts how the Nintendo 3DS, a handheld console engineered with unprecedented security features, was ultimately compromised by a single programming oversight. Despite having a complex architecture designed to be mathematically unhackable, the system fell to a "homebrew" exploit discovered within an obscure, low-budget title called Cubic Ninja. A French hacker exploited a stack buffer overflow in the game's QR code level-sharing feature to bypass the console's digital defenses. This initial breach triggered a years-long cat-and-mouse game between Nintendo and the hacking community, leading to the discovery of unpatchable flaws in the system's hardware and boot code. The narrative highlights a fundamental law of cybersecurity: a fortress is only as secure as its most vulnerable piece of internal software. Ultimately, this history influenced Nintendo to adopt a more resilient security philosophy for its subsequent hardware, the Nintendo Switch.
